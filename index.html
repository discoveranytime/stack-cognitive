Cognitive Stack
Semax + Selank

Semax — the activator, an ACTH-derived peptide studied for focus and stroke recovery. Selank — the calmer, a tuftsin-derived peptide studied for anxiety. Together they're the classic Russian nootropic pair: stimulation without the jitters, calm without the sedation — a combination with no Western approval and thin combo data.

Quick read

The Semax + Selank stack pairs two Russian-developed peptides: Semax (an ACTH fragment analog linked to BDNF and dopamine signaling, a prescription drug in Russia for stroke and cognitive disorders) with Selank (a tuftsin analog with anxiolytic effects comparable to a benzodiazepine in one small trial, sold over the counter in Russia). The forum logic: Semax pushes, Selank steadies — focus plus calm. Both raise BDNF through different routes. Neither is FDA-approved, and the combination itself has no clinical trials.

01The pair

Meet the two partners

Both peptides are seven amino acids long, both were developed in Russia, and both are re-engineered fragments of molecules your body already makes. But they pull the brain in opposite directions — which is exactly the point.

Semax · the activator

A synthetic analog of an ACTH fragment (Met-Glu-His-Phe-Pro-Gly-Pro). Linked to BDNF increases, dopamine and serotonin modulation. A prescription drug in Russia for stroke, head trauma, and cognitive disorders.

Selank · the calmer

A synthetic analog of tuftsin, an immune-system tetrapeptide (Thr-Lys-Pro-Arg-Pro-Gly-Pro). Anxiolytic — calms without sedating — and sold over the counter in Russia since 2017 for anxiety.

The stack · the balance

The pitch: Semax's activation can bring restlessness; Selank's calm offsets the edge while both nudge BDNF upward. A tidy theory — with no combination trials behind it.

Plain English

Think espresso plus a deep breath: Semax is the gas pedal for focus and drive, Selank is the shock absorber for nerves and tension. The stack promises the alertness without the jitters — the oldest balancing act in the stimulant world, now in peptide form.

02Russian origins

Born in Soviet labs

Neither peptide came from a nootropics startup. Both emerged from Russian pharmaceutical research in the late Soviet and post-Soviet era — designed as medicines first, adopted by biohackers decades later.

Medicines in Russia, research chemicals everywhere else.
Approved there, gray-market here

Both are real registered medicines in Russia — which is more than most peptides here can say. But 'registered in Russia' is not FDA approval, and the gray-market vials online are not Russian pharmacy stock.

Intranasal by design

Both were developed as nasal drops/spray — a direct route toward the brain that avoids the gut. The forum world copied the route; the quality control didn't follow.

The enkephalinase footnote

Both peptides appear to slow the breakdown of the brain's own natural opioids (enkephalins) — a mechanism the marketing rarely mentions, and one more reason 'non-addictive' claims deserve scrutiny.

Plain English

These two have actual medical childhoods — hospital wards, stroke units, anxiety clinics — unlike most forum peptides, which were born in a catalog. That history is worth something. It just isn't worth as much as the forums think, once the peptides leave the pharmacy for the gray market.

Why combine them?

Semax · the cognitive push

Experimental neuroactive and neuroprotective research. Proposed: BDNF-related signaling, neurotrophic pathways, oxidative-stress and inflammation effects, and monoaminergic systems — where the evidence supports each piece.

Selank · the stress buffer

Experimental anxiolytic and neuroactive research. Proposed influence on GABAergic regulation and other neurotransmitter systems — the calm half of the pairing.

Complementarity, not synergy

The rationale: Semax is discussed for cognitive, neurotrophic, and neuroprotective effects; Selank for anxiolytic, stress-related, and neuroregulatory effects. Conceptually, Semax supports cognitive/neurotrophic signaling while Selank potentially reduces stress or anxiety-related interference. That is a theory of complementarity — not proven clinical synergy. No trial has tested the combination.

How quickly, how long

Users commonly report acute changes — sharper focus, calmer nerves — within roughly 15–60 minutes, while steadier benefits are described over days to weeks of regular use. Reported timelines, not guarantees: individual responses vary widely, and no trial has measured the combination's time course.

04Evidence

What the science actually supports

By peptide-forum standards, the evidence file here is unusually real — Russian clinical literature exists. By medical standards, it's still thin: small trials, one regulatory tradition, no Western replication.

Semax: stroke & BDNF

Strongest human data is in Russian stroke research, where it's an approved treatment. Mechanistic work on BDNF is suggestive. Western replication: essentially none.

Selank: the 62-patient trial

Compared against medazepam (a benzodiazepine) in generalized anxiety: comparable anxiolysis with anti-fatigue, stimulating effects — and no sedation, dependence, or cognitive dulling in the available research. Small, Russian, but a real active-comparator trial.

The stack: nothing

No published trial of Semax + Selank together — not for cognition, not for anything. The most popular nootropic peptide pairing has no pairing data.

Why these are not traditional stimulants

Caffeine

Blocks adenosine receptors — a borrowed-energy mechanism with a well-known crash. Neither peptide works this way.

Amphetamine-type stimulants

Force monoamine release and reuptake blockade — powerful, with real dependence and neurotoxicity risk. No peptide here does this.

Conventional anxiolytics

Benzodiazepines bind GABA-A directly and sedate. Selank's anxiolysis is proposed through gene regulation — a different route, different evidence bar.

No equivalence implied

Contrasting mechanisms is not ranking them. The stimulants above have decades of human pharmacology behind them — the peptides do not, and "different mechanism" is not automatically "better."

08Who it may suit

Who it may suit — and who it should not

Who is this evidence file actually relevant to — and who should be extra careful? General information about the research landscape, not personal medical advice.

✅ Who it may be relevant to

  • Russian clinicians — Semax is a registered prescription option there for stroke recovery and cognitive disorders; Selank for anxiety. These are established indications — in Russia.
  • Researchers studying BDNF signaling — both peptides are linked to BDNF through different routes, a genuine mechanistic thread.
  • People comparing nootropic evidence files — this pair has the most real clinical history of any stack in this series.
  • Anyone weighing "registered somewhere" vs "approved here" — the distinction this whole page turns on.
  • Students and professionals under heavy mental load — the "calm focus" use case the stack is most often discussed for, without classic stimulants.
  • People exploring non-stimulant options who understand the Western evidence is thin and the combination itself is untested.

❌ Who it may NOT suit / extra caution

  • Anyone expecting proven cognitive enhancement — IQ and memory gains are unproven and speculative.
  • People sensitive to stimulation — Semax's activating edge can mean restlessness, irritability, or disrupted sleep.
  • Anyone on psychiatric medications, blood thinners, or neurological agents — the file contains no interaction data (and the enkephalinase effects complicate breezy 'non-habit-forming' claims), so medical supervision is needed.
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding individuals, children and adolescents — no safety data exists for these groups.
  • People with uncontrolled psychiatric conditions, active nasal disease or frequent nosebleeds, uncontrolled cardiovascular issues, or known hypersensitivity to these peptides — higher risk, thinner data.
  • Buyers of gray-market vials — online products are not Russian pharmacy stock; nasal delivery sends whatever is in the bottle straight toward the brain.
  • Everyone, on the combination — Semax + Selank together has no safety or efficacy data. Two mild profiles don't make a mild pair.
Plain English

Registered indications are geography-specific: a Russian stroke patient and a forum buyer are reading two different evidence files. This is general information, not personal medical advice — brain-chemistry decisions belong with a licensed clinician.

09Risks & unknowns

Risks & unknowns

Russian registration means some safety data exists — more than most peptides here. It doesn't cover gray-market vials, Western populations in trials, or the combination.

Known

Generally well tolerated (in trials)

Observed: Russian clinical use reports mostly mild effects — nasal irritation, dryness, stinging, runny nose, or sneezing from the sprays; occasional headache, mild fatigue, lightheadedness, subtle mood shifts, or sleep changes if used late in the day. Serious reactions appear rare in available reports — but individual responses vary, and none of this covers gray-market products or the combination.

Theoretical

The enkephalinase question

Theoretical: both peptides slow the breakdown of endogenous opioids. That may contribute to mood effects — and it complicates breezy 'non-habit-forming' claims.

Unknown

Gray-market quality

Unknown: what's sold online as Semax/Selank is unregulated. Wrong dose, impurities, or wrong compound entirely — nasal products add direct-to-brain delivery of whatever's actually in the bottle.

Unknown

The combination

Unknown: no safety data exists for Semax + Selank together. Two 'mild' profiles don't automatically make a mild pair.

Theoretical

Stimulant edge

Theoretical: Semax's activating character can mean restlessness, irritability, or sleep disruption in some users — the exact problem Selank is supposed to solve, per forum theory.

Known

Not FDA-evaluated

Observed fact: neither peptide has been evaluated or approved by Western regulators. Russian registration is real but follows a different evidence tradition.

Plain English

Known: mild in the clinic, mostly. Unknown: everything about the gray-market versions and the combination. The stack borrows a safety reputation from Russian pharmacies and spends it in unregulated bottles.

10Terminology

Understanding Nootropic Terminology

Twelve terms, each with a scientific definition and a plain-English translation. Tap any term to expand it.

The scienceSemax is a synthetic heptapeptide with the sequence Met-Glu-His-Phe-Pro-Gly-Pro, modeled on a fragment of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH 4–7) with a stabilizing Pro-Gly-Pro tail. It was developed in Russia, where it is registered as a prescription medication for neurological conditions. Laboratory and clinical research associate it with increased brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and modulation of dopaminergic signaling, though its precise molecular mechanism remains incompletely characterized. In registered use it is administered intranasally.

Plain EnglishSemax is a lab-made brain peptide based on a tiny piece of the body's stress hormone, with a chemical tail added to make it last longer. It was developed in Russia, where doctors can actually prescribe it for brain-related conditions. Scientists link it to higher levels of BDNF, a protein that helps brain cells grow and stay flexible, plus effects on the brain's dopamine system — but the exact way it works is still not fully pinned down. Where it is used medically, it goes in through the nose, not as a pill.

The scienceSelank is a synthetic heptapeptide (Thr-Lys-Pro-Arg-Pro-Gly-Pro) modeled on tuftsin, a naturally occurring immunoregulatory tetrapeptide. It was developed in Russia, where it is registered as a prescription anxiolytic. Its distinguishing property is anxiety reduction without the sedation, dependence, or withdrawal typically associated with benzodiazepines. Proposed mechanisms include shifts in GABA-related gene expression and inhibition of enkephalin-degrading enzymes, rather than direct receptor binding. In registered use it is administered intranasally.

Plain EnglishSelank is a lab-made peptide copied from tuftsin, a small immune-system signal the body already makes. Russia developed and registered it as a prescription anti-anxiety drug. What makes it stand out is that it calms anxiety without making you drowsy or carrying the dependence and withdrawal baggage of benzodiazepines. Instead of locking directly onto brain receptors, it seems to nudge which calming-related genes get switched on and slow down the enzymes that chew up the brain's natural opioid signals. Medically, it is given through the nose.

The scienceBrain-derived neurotrophic factor is a protein of the neurotrophin family that supports the growth, survival, and differentiation of neurons. It plays a central role in synaptic plasticity — the brain's ability to strengthen connections — and is involved in learning and memory. BDNF levels are reduced in several neuropsychiatric conditions and can be raised by exercise, learning, and certain pharmacological agents. Both Semax and Selank have been reported to elevate BDNF expression in research settings.

Plain EnglishBDNF is a protein your brain makes that keeps neurons alive, helps them grow, and lets connections between them strengthen — it is a big part of how learning and memory work physically. Low levels show up in depression, anxiety, and cognitive decline, while exercise reliably raises it. A lot of nootropic research circles around BDNF because more of it generally means a more adaptable brain. Both Semax and Selank have been linked to higher BDNF in studies — that is one reason the pair is combined.

The scienceTuftsin is a naturally occurring tetrapeptide (Thr-Lys-Pro-Arg) derived from the Fc region of immunoglobulin G antibodies. It stimulates phagocytosis and motility in macrophages and neutrophils, making it an immunoregulatory signal rather than a neural one. Selank is a synthetic analog that extends the tuftsin sequence with a Pro-Gly-Pro tail, which prolongs its resistance to enzymatic breakdown. It is the parent structure from which Selank's anxiolytic design was built.

Plain EnglishTuftsin is a tiny four-part molecule your immune system already makes from antibody fragments. Its job is to wake up immune cells like macrophages and neutrophils so they eat invaders more aggressively — it is an immune message, not a brain chemical. Selank is a longer-lived, lab-built cousin of tuftsin, with an extra tail that stops it breaking down as fast. So despite being used for calm and focus, Selank's blueprint comes from the immune system, not the nervous system.

The scienceAdrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) is a pituitary hormone that stimulates the adrenal cortex to release cortisol, the body's primary stress hormone. Semax copies only residues 4 through 7 of ACTH's chain and adds a stabilizing Pro-Gly-Pro tail. The fragment lacks the full hormone's cortisol-driving activity while retaining some of the parent molecule's neural effects. Its presence in a cognitive peptide surprises people because ACTH is famous for the stress response.

Plain EnglishACTH is the hormone your pituitary gland sends to the adrenals to trigger cortisol — the classic stress signal. Semax does not copy the whole hormone; it takes just four links of its chain (positions 4 to 7) and adds a stabilizing tail. That short piece cannot drive cortisol the way full ACTH does, but it keeps some of the parent's brain-related activity. It is a reminder that a molecule famous for stress can be repurposed into something used for focus.

The scienceAn anxiolytic is any substance or intervention that reduces anxiety. The category spans benzodiazepines, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, buspirone, and various experimental compounds. Benzodiazepines act quickly but commonly cause sedation, dependence, and withdrawal. Selank's headline property is its reported anxiolytic effect without those sedating and dependence liabilities — an unusual profile within the class.

Plain EnglishAnxiolytic is just the technical word for anything that reduces anxiety — from benzodiazepines to SSRIs to buspirone. The catch with the classic fast-acting ones, the benzos, is that they tend to make you drowsy and can be hard to stop. Selank's claim to fame is calming anxiety without the sedation and without the dependence baggage. That is what makes it unusual in this category — if the effect holds up.

The scienceEnkephalinases are enzymes — chiefly neutral endopeptidase and aminopeptidase N — that break down enkephalins, the brain's own opioid peptides. By degrading these natural painkillers and mood modulators, the enzymes limit how long enkephalin signaling lasts. Both Semax and Selank are reported to inhibit these enzymes, which would prolong the activity of the body's endogenous opioid signals. This proposed mechanism is distinct from activating opioid receptors directly.

Plain EnglishEnkephalinases are cleanup enzymes that break down enkephalins — your brain's natural, mild opioid-like chemicals that ease pain and lift mood. Because they get chewed up fast, their effects are short-lived. Both Semax and Selank are reported to slow these cleanup enzymes down, which would stretch out the signal of the natural opioids you already make. It is a back-door route: not adding opioids, just making your own last longer.

The scienceGamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) is the principal inhibitory neurotransmitter of the central nervous system, reducing neuronal excitability throughout the brain. Most fast-acting anti-anxiety drugs, including benzodiazepines, work by enhancing signaling at GABA-A receptors. Selank does not appear to bind these receptors directly. Instead, research suggests it shifts the expression of genes involved in GABA signaling — an indirect route to a similar calming effect.

Plain EnglishGABA is your brain's main brake-pedal chemical — it quiets overactive neurons and keeps the whole system from firing out of control. Most quick anti-anxiety drugs, like benzos, work by turning up GABA signaling directly at the receptor. Selank seems to take a different path: rather than grabbing the receptor, it nudges the genes that control GABA machinery up or down. Same destination — a calmer brain — reached indirectly.

The scienceNeurasthenia is a historical diagnostic concept describing chronic fatigue, weakness, irritability, and difficulty concentrating. It fell out of use in most Western psychiatry during the twentieth century as its symptoms were redistributed into depression, anxiety, and fatigue disorders. It remains a recognized diagnosis in Russian and some Eastern European medical practice. Neurasthenia is one of Selank's registered indications in Russia.

Plain EnglishNeurasthenia is an old-fashioned medical label for a mix of exhaustion, weakness, irritability, and brain fog. Western psychiatry mostly retired the term decades ago, splitting its symptoms into depression, anxiety, and chronic fatigue diagnoses. But Russian medicine still uses it as a real diagnosis — and it is one of the official indications on Selank's Russian label. It reads like a period piece, but it is live medical vocabulary there.

The scienceIntranasal administration delivers a substance through the nasal mucosa rather than by mouth or injection. It is the registered route for both Semax and Selank in Russia. Nasal delivery avoids gastrointestinal breakdown and first-pass metabolism in the liver, and the olfactory region offers close anatomical proximity to the brain. These properties make the nose a practical shortcut for peptide drugs that would not survive digestion.

Plain EnglishIntranasal just means delivered through the nose — and it is the official route for both Semax and Selank where they are registered. Peptides get destroyed by stomach acid and digestive enzymes, so a pill would be pointless; the nasal lining absorbs them directly into the bloodstream instead. The nose also sits right under the brain, which is why it is the chosen delivery route for these compounds. That is why they come as sprays or drops, never as tablets.

The scienceA nootropic is a substance claimed to enhance cognitive functions such as memory, focus, or creativity with minimal side effects. The term was coined by the pharmacologist Corneliu Giurgea in 1972 to describe a strict set of criteria that few compounds meet. In practice it has become a loose marketing category rather than a pharmacological one, applied to everything from caffeine to experimental peptides. It describes an aspiration, not a proven mechanism.

Plain English‘Smart drug’ is the street name for the same idea. A scientist named Corneliu Giurgea invented the word ‘nootropic’ in 1972 with a strict definition — almost nothing on the market actually meets his original criteria. In practice, the label has become a marketing shelf where anything promising better focus gets placed. It is a promise category, not a proven one — the word tells you what a product claims, not what it does.

The scienceAn active comparator is a trial design in which an experimental treatment is tested against an existing approved treatment rather than against placebo. Demonstrating superiority or non-inferiority to a real drug is generally stronger evidence than beating a sugar pill. Selank's oft-cited 62-patient anxiety trial used an active comparator design, finding effects comparable to a benzodiazepine with a stimulating rather than sedating character.

Plain EnglishIn most drug trials, the new treatment is tested against a placebo — a sugar pill. An active comparator trial skips that and tests the new drug against a real, existing medication instead. Beating or matching an actual drug is a tougher bar and produces more convincing evidence. Selank's best-known anxiety trial used this tougher design: 62 patients, and its effects were comparable to a benzodiazepine — but stimulating rather than sedating.
